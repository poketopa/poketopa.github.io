### The problem with transcription

Initially, Knot's models took <strong>about 15\~35 minutes per hour of audio</strong> to transcribe. <br />This varied with the amount of speech in the recording, averaging around 25 minutes.<br />I thought receiving a transcript only twenty minutes after a one-hour meeting was <strong>not a pleasant experience</strong>. <br />Other typical transcription services finish within thirty seconds after recording ends.

In PostHog, I repeatedly saw users <strong>wait about a minute after ending a recording, then leave the page</strong>.

<p class="rilog-empty-block" aria-hidden="true"><br /></p>

This post records our thinking and results as we sought ways to reduce transcription time while maintaining the quality of Knot's models.

## 1\. Transcribing while recording

An earlier change meant the browser was already saving audio to S3 every five seconds. <br />Audio was arriving at the server before meetings ended.

But we combined all the audio and transcribed it in one go only after recording ended.

<em><strong>What if we transcribed this audio first?</strong></em> <br />After recording ended, we could leave only the final segment to process and the earlier results to connect.

![](/images/rilog/358/01.webp)

Even without making the model itself faster, processing transcription while users were meeting could dramatically reduce the time until results appeared after recording ended.

## 2\. How much audio should we collect before transcribing?

Saving every five seconds did not mean we could feed five-second chunks to the model. <br />Short inputs cut speech off midway and provided <strong>insufficient context</strong>. <br />Conversely, collecting audio for too long delayed the start of transcription.

So we compared configurations using a recording of the same meeting with a reference transcript. We experimented with <strong>30-second, 2-minute, and 3-minute</strong> segments. Shorter segments produced results sooner after recording ended, but introduced a risk of higher error rates because there were more cut points.

| Input configuration | Character error rate |
| --- | --- |
| Previous 30-second segment configuration | 10.2% |
| 2-minute segment configuration | 8.3% |
| 3-minute segments with the same settings | 8.2% |

The 30-second and 2-minute configurations also differed in context and merging methods, so not all of the difference could be attributed to segment length. Comparing 2-minute and 3-minute segments with the same settings showed a difference of 0.1 percentage points.

Since the error rates for 2-minute and 3-minute segments were nearly identical, we judged that the quality difference did not justify another minute's wait and decided to <strong>transcribe in 2-minute segments</strong>.

### Speech being cut off at segment boundaries

There is no guarantee that a sentence ends at the two-minute mark.

For example, if a segment boundary occurs while a user is saying `안녕하세요` (hello), the previous input contains only `안녕` and the next only `하세요`. When transcribed separately, the model never hears the continuous utterance. Without enough surrounding context, it could recognize different words or omit parts.

Simply joining segment transcripts afterward could not easily undo words already misrecognized. I thought we needed to <strong>let the model hear the beginning of the cut-off speech when transcribing the next segment</strong>.

So from the second segment onward, we added <strong>the preceding ten seconds as overlap</strong> to the new two minutes of audio.

![](/images/rilog/358/02.webp)

![](/images/rilog/358/03.webp)

The first input spans 0–120 seconds, and the next 110–240 seconds. <br />Since it reuses previously recorded audio, no additional waiting for the next audio was necessary.

Simply joining the overlapping parts could duplicate the same words. <br />So we aligned word timestamps to the original audio and compared word order and timing in the overlap to connect the results.

## 3\. Reducing unnecessary waiting

We had reduced model processing time to speed up transcription, but breaking down the time in our development environment showed that the backend also spent time collecting stored audio into a file to send to AI.

Investigating revealed that it was <strong>continually updating state in the DB</strong> whenever it copied a small piece of audio. This was necessary so another worker could take over if one stopped, but a separate job already updated it at regular intervals. Copying files repeated the same work and waited for the DB response.

We left DB updates to the separate periodic job and changed file copying to check only an in-memory stop flag.<br />If an update failed, the stop flag would change, and the copy task would detect it and stop.

```java
TranscriptionWindowAudioAssembler.MaterializedWindowAudio audio = assembler.materialize(
        source.orElseThrow(),
        () -> ensureActive(owned)
);
checkpoint(claim, owned);

private void ensureActive(AtomicBoolean owned) {
    if (!accepting || !owned.get() || Thread.currentThread().isInterrupted()) {
        throw new RecordingException(RecordingErrorCode.AI_INTERRUPTED);
    }
}
```

## 4\. The results?

Even with a shorter wait, the approach would be difficult to use if transcription quality dropped significantly. <br />So we compared transcribing the same audio in one go with transcribing and joining two-minute segments.

| Processing method | Aggregate character error rate |
| --- | --- |
| Whole-file transcription | 10.2% |
| 2-minute segment transcription | 10.8% |

<strong>In this evaluation, the character error rate differed by 0.6 percentage points.</strong> The error rate was slightly higher with two-minute segments, but we judged that the difference did not justify waiting for the whole meeting to be transcribed again.<br />Based on these results, we <strong>used the segment results produced during recording directly as the final transcript</strong>.

### Improvement and limitations

With the improved approach, however many hours we record, we only wait for the final transcription segment, taking about thirty seconds after recording ends. Compared with more than twenty minutes for a one-hour recording, this amounts to <strong>about a 97%</strong> <strong>improvement</strong>.

However, simultaneous recordings revealed another problem. When multiple people recorded, jobs piled up in a queue like tasks for a CPU scheduler. We observed transcription falling further behind when jobs accumulated faster than the Mac mini could transcribe them. Throughput for simultaneous recordings remained our next improvement task.
