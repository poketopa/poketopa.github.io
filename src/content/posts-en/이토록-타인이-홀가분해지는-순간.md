I like a small circle of deep relationships.

And I think I took some pride in that.

So I always told my friends: you are my true friend.

Saying that let me make them feel special, and maintaining a small circle was easier for me.

But did those words make them special, or pressure them to treat me as equally special?

### Because we are human
People gain different things from their relationships: comfort from family, enjoyment and freedom from friends, recognition and a sense of growth from colleagues, and love and stability from partners. These are essential human needs, and each relationship can offer us different things.

The problem is that reducing our relationships does not reduce those needs. Even when we see fewer people, we still need to feel loved, recognized, and connected to someone. Eventually, we expect more than before from the few people who remain.

We ask one friend to fill the roles once shared by ten, and ask one partner to provide what family, friends, and colleagues used to provide together. Relationships become simpler, but the responsibility borne by the person within them becomes heavier.

`You are all I have` sounds like a romantic confession. But sometimes it also means `So you have to fulfill everything I need.`

### What kind of person was I?
Looking back, I think I unconsciously pressured the people around me and expected the same level of treatment in return.

I deliberately narrowed my circle, continually building expectations and then feeling disappointed. I envied them when they got along with new friends as well as they did with me. The closer someone was to me, the more I reacted to every change in their mood and became dependent.

As it worsened, I even felt guilty about working hard to grow and achieve things.

That may sound strange. To explain, I have a strong sense of inferiority. Even when someone is close to me, it does not feel particularly pleasant when they show themselves to be better than me.

So I think I assumed others would be similar. I wondered whether they would hate me if I surpassed them. Even the phrase "surpass someone" seems like evidence that I had become consumed by something. I thought my growth might make the other person feel helpless or inferior.

But these were not feelings they had actually experienced; I was merely projecting my own.

I do not think it was healthy at all.

What I wanted was for the people I cherished to be happier when they were with me, remember me as a good person, and seek me out often.

### Dependence means shifting the center, rather than severing the connection
They say the closer someone is to us, the more time and emotion we have invested, making it difficult to change anything in the relationship. We cannot easily make less room for them in our hearts or cut them off. Trying itself is difficult.

And entrusting the love, recognition, growth, stability, and belonging I need solely to those closest to me is harmful to both me and them.

Instead of indiscriminately cutting off dependence in relationships, the book tells us to widen our circle. It says that as we make new connections beyond our existing circle and increase the small relationships that fulfill our needs, dependence naturally lessens and its center shifts.

### If you want to help, become a pillar

The oxygen mask analogy that had impressed me before appeared again in this book.

It asks who should put on an oxygen mask first in an in-flight emergency: the parent or the child.

It seems obvious that the child should go first, but on a plane, the instructions say the parent should put theirs on first.

Because only the parent can put the child's oxygen mask on. If the parent has their mask on, the child will soon have theirs on too, but the reverse is not true.

In relationships too, the way to protect those I cherish is to protect myself first and become their pillar, rather than blindly expecting things from them or devoting myself to them.
