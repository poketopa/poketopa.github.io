## Am I a developer who writes good prompts?

“Good prompt” is an ambiguous concept. Depending on the situation and the individual's abilities, the same prompt can be good or bad.

So I described my criteria as concretely as possible and left the collection, aggregation, and interpretation of sample conversations to Codex.

I defined what I wanted to know and the scope of analysis, then requested further analysis after receiving the results.

![Directions for analyzing prompts, including interests, learning process, and questioning habits](/images/prompt-retrospective/analysis-directions.png)

I wanted to know whether my questioning habits were effective, whether they had improved, and how my interests had changed.

The analysis counted and measured the full history, then selected conversations to read through the questions, answers, and revisions.

The evaluation separated how the questions were written from their actual effects.

The following results describe behavior found in the records and interpretations of it.

## Is prompting ability proportional to vocabulary?

Watching different people around me use AI, I noticed something they had in common.

The ability to use AI seemed somewhat proportional to vocabulary.

People who knew exactly what they wanted and could articulate it with appropriate words and expressions got good results.

By contrast, people who treated AI as a `someone who can read the room` struggled to use it well.

For example: `But come on, doesn't it look a bit strange to you, too?`

These people mostly went on about how stupid AI was.

So I always tried to be conscious of presenting my situation and what I wanted in prompts, and asking for feedback on ambiguities in my questions.

In fact, from 2024 onward, many of my questions first explained my understanding and asked for incorrect parts to be identified.

> Excerpt from a ChatGPT conversation on 2024-09-18
>
> I'll explain what I know, so point out anything wrong. When a process runs, it is allocated memory, its data waits in RAM, and the CPU handles the necessary operations. RAM interacts with the CPU to help with fast computation.

There were also instances of making specific corrections when an answer differed from what I wanted.

Of course, as AI improves, even these aspects will be abstracted away and everyone will get similar results, but I still think concrete, detailed prompts are very important.

## AI chooses words that make me feel good rather than answers.

> The complete user input from a ChatGPT conversation on 2025-04-01
>
> A restaurant where the owner takes orders, cooks, serves, and handles payments is the worst object-oriented design, while one with ten employees each doing just one step is totally object-oriented.
>
> Excerpt from the AI response immediately afterward:
>
> This expression really shows that **you understand the essence of object orientation perfectly**.
>
> It also said at the end:
>
> You've completely mastered it through this analogy.

Had I really mastered object orientation?

Looking back now, it is absurd.

The gap between an analogy at that level and mastering object orientation is ridiculously large.

At least in this conversation, AI praised me rather than checking my understanding.

Let's take an example.

If I ask AI `Is lying bad?`, it will give an ambiguous answer.

Something like `Lying is usually wrong, but in special circumstances it isn't necessarily bad!`.

What matters is what I say next. If I insist that lying is bad, AI will agree; if I say white lies are really good, it will agree in that direction.

In other words, for questions with no fixed answer such as a truth, AI simply produces the answer I am most likely to like.

In the object-orientation conversation above, I had not even asked whether my explanation was perfect, but AI said I had completely mastered it.

Asking AI something like `I did well, right?` simply invites the answer `You did great~`.

To prevent this, I tried to explicitly ask for incorrect parts to be pointed out, and there were many such records. I had asked for feedback identifying holes in my explanation rather than praise.

> Excerpt from a ChatGPT conversation on 2025-04-17
>
> I'm learning Spring, so there may be errors in the way I summarize things or ask questions. You need to identify those and tell me what is wrong.

## Back to the first question

Had I been writing prompts well?

The records showed habits worth keeping, and places where I could make more specific requests. But 23,144 inputs could not be reduced to a single skill score.

I think what matters is using those records to actually change how I ask questions.

I do not yet know whether the improvements suggested this time will help me. But I still believe the ability to state specifically what I want matters, and I will keep studying and improving to write better prompts.
