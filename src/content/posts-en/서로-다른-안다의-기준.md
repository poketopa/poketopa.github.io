I thought trust was the most important thing in a project.

Since teammates divide up roles and do different work, I thought I needed to believe that `I can trust what my teammates say` even about work I was not involved in.

So I constantly questioned where my knowledge ended and my ignorance began.

If I did not know something precisely, I did not actively express an opinion. I only spoke up when I was certain.

But communicating during the project revealed the problems with that approach.

## 48 tables, 146 issues

Knot's original plan included Notion's editor features.

So a teammate and I studied Notion's data structure and tried to apply it to our service.

Notion handles page contents in blocks, and the structure was more complex and difficult than expected.

As we tried to represent not just pages and blocks but also text formatting, references, databases, and properties in our service, the things we needed to consider kept growing.

My teammate drew up a very detailed structure early in the project and argued that representing Notion's structure would require many tables.

![](/images/rilog/262/01.webp)

![](/images/rilog/262/02.webp)

We ended up designing 48 tables as soon as the project started, and the number of open issues in the repository grew to 146.

For a project whose direction was still unclear, the structure was bizarrely large.

I only had an instinctive sense that something was wrong.

But it was just an instinct, and I could not express it logically.

I could not explain why we should not have so many tables, what problems would arise, or how we should design it instead.

I worried that objecting without evidence would look like I was blocking someone's hard work based on a feeling, so I agreed with my teammate's direction.

I thought my doubts came from not understanding the structure well enough.

I was afraid that speaking up prematurely would lead us to a bad judgment.

But the fact that I held back does not seem to mean we made a better judgment.

## Unresolved resentment

Separate from the bad judgment, what accumulated at a frightening pace was my resentment toward the project.

My doubts about the decision did not disappear; they grew in increasingly negative directions. I badly wanted to identify and correct what was wrong, but I was not confident I could persuade my teammate with airtight reasoning.

I felt that the trust I mentioned earlier, the most important thing in a team project, was crumbling.

Looking back, from my teammate's perspective, I probably seemed like someone who agreed outwardly without sharing what was actually bothering me.

From my perspective, I held back for the sake of trust, but failing to express my opinion ultimately amounted to standing by and doing nothing.

## An answer from a coach

Later, talking with a coach, I heard that we should be able to explain designs and technologies starting from the user's problem.

The coach's advice answered my earlier feeling that something was off.

We could not answer why we needed a particular table now, why we had to do this much in the current sprint, or which user's problem it solved.

Afterward, I felt this was a conclusion we could have reached without much difficulty if we had tried having more conversations earlier.

Even if my thoughts were incomplete, expressing what I felt and aligning our opinions and knowledge could have helped us find an answer and make a better choice.

Looking back, I was trying to have too much in place before voicing an opinion.

I thought I had to explain the problem precisely, know what it would cause, and even propose an alternative design.

## What counts as 'knowing'

My biggest realization through this process was that people have different standards for when they can say they 'know' something.

Some say they know something when they can explain it completely; others say so after understanding only the basic flow.

These differences can cause clashes and conflict in a project where ideas are exchanged.

During the design phase, I thought I knew nothing while my teammate was in a position to make many judgments. But our conversations revealed that our understanding at the time was similar, and in some areas mine was greater.

The problem was that we judged each other's level by the degree of confidence, without first understanding the depth of each other's knowledge.

## My resolution

I learned that if I say nothing until I understand something perfectly, doubts I have not yet organized never even get a chance to be discussed.

Now, during discussions, I try to state clearly what I know and where my knowledge ends.

Even if I cannot yet explain the cause, I will try not to hide the fact that I have a question.
