## 1. A generation that hides so much

---

The book says that over half of people in their twenties and thirties regard negative emotions such as anger and despair as things to hide.

The idea is that younger generations are highly independent and do not want to show their feelings to others.

At the same time, it says that content that feels emotions on their behalf has recently become popular among millennials and Gen Z.

I found this strangely unpleasant.

Why do people who think they should hide their emotions enjoy consuming content that expresses anger for them?

Why do younger generations feel lost, and why are there so many people who lump them together as 'MZ' and point fingers at them?

It was similar to the discomfort I felt when the term 'young forty' became an issue.

Older generations criticize millennials and Gen Z for putting individuals before the organization, and millennials and Gen Z respond with terms mocking those older generations.

I know that this kind of generational rhetoric and hatred is a typical problem in countries that have undergone rapid industrialization and growth.

Even so, is there no helping the resentment toward reality and the hatred of others that wells up?

Why are we so lost?

I think phenomena prominent in a particular generation are dominated by the influence of education and social ideology, rather than by characteristics of a stage of human life such as age.

Considering that education can suppress even human instinct, our younger generation seems confused by the mismatch between what we have been taught since childhood and what is demanded by the society into which we are suddenly thrown.

In the past, Korea's task was to quickly follow paths already laid by countries further ahead.

For our parents' generation, efficiency mattered more than creativity, execution more than curiosity, and someone who could get things done quickly was the ideal talent of the time.

Effort meant reward, and effort meant success.

But Korea has now joined the ranks of developed nations, and the answer sheet we have been following has disappeared.

High growth rates are gone, and there is no guarantee at all that our efforts will bring proportional returns.

Can a generation taught only how to solve problems, forced to compete by stepping on others and to strive for perfection, be happy in an age when only those who create creative problems are celebrated, and AI threatens not only jobs but human existence itself?

Perhaps what we saw reflected back at us was the image of the loser we had so desperately wanted to avoid becoming.

It could never be easy for people in that position to express their feelings honestly.

Projecting the image and standards of a successful person that have been instilled in us, we so easily criticize people who do not measure up and condemn other generations as a group for having been born into favorable times.

Probably because that is the easiest way to attack and rationalize.

These conflicts born of historical circumstances are very difficult to resolve.

I know the answer ultimately lies in empathizing with and understanding the limitations and pain of each generation's era.

I know all I can do is do my best at what is within my control and move forward.

Still, accepting the emptiness and resentment that suddenly rise up is not easy.

## 2. What it means to be human

---

Until now, humanity has tried to move away from 'being human.'

We wanted greater efficiency, thought, memory, and computation, and advances in engineering gave us those things.

Because humans evolved so inefficiently, we tried to overcome that through technology.

Perhaps we considered that overcoming to be the path to perfection.

Even so, technology could not replace the human essence of thinking and creating.

But what about AI today?

It has entered human territory to a chilling depth and shows us something close to the perfection we want so badly.

Paradoxically, seeing this has led us to ask what our essence is.

In a future where AI possesses superintelligence and frees humanity from labor, will people really be happier than now?

Following these questions one after another, I become convinced that value in human life ultimately comes from what we lack.

I think all our joys and sorrows—happiness, love, sadness, effort, achievement—and life's precious meanings arise from life's finiteness and individual imperfection.

In the Netflix series Arcane, which I watched recently, Viktor says that humanity becoming a single intelligence and overcoming imperfection and finiteness is perfection, the glorious evolution of the species.

But after achieving all of that, Viktor realizes he was completely wrong and tries to correct the past.

I think the reason the ultimate perfection we pursue is tragic is also related to the lack I mentioned earlier.

What would humanity look like after completely transcending the emotional pain and diseases that torment us, and our bodies and minds that remain stuck in the Stone Age without evolving, and mastering the workings of the universe?

At least one thing is certain: they would be profoundly static beings who neither explore anything new nor feel emotions, nor even need to.

Then how is that different from what we think of as death?

Perhaps the perfection humanity pursues ultimately resembles death—in other words, something inanimate.

And perhaps what we feel toward AI is the same kind of fear we feel toward such a death.

The fact this book describes, that 'more people are trying to care for themselves and find small pleasures,'

Might be people's attempt to escape perfection and rediscover the preciousness that comes from what we lack.
