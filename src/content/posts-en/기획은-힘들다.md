In Level 3 of Woowa Tech Course, we were given a mission to plan a service idea that people would actually use. What came to my mind was a harness.

This means preparing documents such as `AGENTS.md` and work instructions so that AI coding tools can work with reference to a project's rules and context. I wanted to build a service that helped teams document and apply their development conventions in this way.

Not many crew members around me knew what a harness was.

I thought that gap offered an opportunity.

Even if learning about and building a harness yourself is a hassle, wouldn't people use a service that made it easy to apply one by answering a few questions?

So I built Harness Initializer.

## My service idea

It asked what a team should agree on first when starting a Java and Spring project, then turned their choices into documents AI coding tools could read. It explained where to install the generated files and helped users apply them easily.

![Harness Initializer service screen](/images/harness-initializer/landing.png)

![Harness Initializer result document screen](/images/harness-initializer/result.png)

Team members had different development convention preferences, and rules agreed on verbally were not followed consistently during development.

We also had to explain the same rules to AI repeatedly.

In my mind, this was a service that would make projects easier.

But the crew members did not see it that way.

## Not knowing something and needing it are different?

I showed the prototype and conducted interviews. Many crew members agreed that the problem existed. But that did not translate into as much interest in using the service as I had expected.

Looking back at their responses, I saw two groups.

People who clearly understood the need for a harness were already using an `AGENTS.md` or rules file tailored to their projects.

Rather than trusting the harness my service generated as it was, they considered a harness they had built themselves, based on their own understanding of the project, more suitable. Easy document generation was not a sufficient reason for them. There was little reason to entrust their project's rules to a service that did something they already knew how to do.

For people unfamiliar with harnesses, on the other hand, I thought I simply needed to make creating and applying one easy to understand.

But some of them did not want to use AI coding tools actively, or had no interest in harnesses at all. I felt they were thinking, "Why should I use this service when I don't particularly feel any inconvenience?"

I had assumed they did not use harnesses because they were difficult to learn and apply. But many of the people I met did not feel the need for them in the first place.

Those who needed one were already solving the problem their own way, while those who did not know about it needed an explanation of why it mattered before an explanation of the solution.

## When things do not follow my train of thought

On July 27, I wrote that I would abandon the idea for the service I was building.

My confidence in being able to attract users fell from 6 to 2.

Looking back, I expected users to follow the same sequence of reasoning that had convinced me. Harnesses are helpful. Building one yourself is a hassle. If a service makes it easy, people will use it.

It felt natural to me. But for people already using one, whether they could trust my generated documents mattered more. For those not using one, the first question was whether they needed it at all.

Planning a service required going one step beyond translating my own train of thought into a product.
