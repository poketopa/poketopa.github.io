People experience declines in judgment and cognitive capacity because of various kinds of scarcity.

Money, business, time, loneliness, and relationships can all be sources of this scarcity.

People experiencing scarcity fall into tunneling: they focus on finding ways to resolve the immediate shortage in front of them and cannot anticipate the chain of consequences that follows.

Of course, people who lack something must do their best to resolve it, but these solutions always involve tradeoffs.

For example, financially poor people can borrow money to address their shortage, but they incur interest obligations, which become the tradeoff of addressing that scarcity.

Yet poor people are generally likely to lack the means to cover the principal and interest, and often do not even recognize this fact.

Paradoxically, actions taken to escape scarcity make the person fall deeper into it.

Escaping this vicious cycle requires slack that can absorb the shocks of scarcity.

In the earlier example, having spare funds for sudden expenses could provide that slack.

Of course, the deeper someone is in scarcity, the less likely they are to have such slack.

Ultimately, a pattern emerges in which poorer people act less wisely and make fewer plans for the future.

The authors say we should understand the strong correlation between poverty and failure as poverty causing failure, rather than simply accepting that people are poor because they are foolish, but the book does not provide an adequate solution.

Supporting this are statistics showing that when poor people received enough income to escape scarcity, almost none tried to invest part of it to seek higher future income.

Because the decline in cognitive capacity caused by scarcity is severe enough to easily consume temporary slack.
