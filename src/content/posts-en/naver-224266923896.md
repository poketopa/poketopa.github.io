## Soft skills

Why do soft skills matter?

Why emphasize them so much even in development and coding, where logic, ideas, performance, and results matter?

Surprisingly, the reason lies in human nature.

People fear personal criticism more than they value their own or their group's growth, leading them to act irrationally and undermine efficiency.

Even when someone believes an idea is their best, fear of attack makes them present it hidden among other ideas they do not care about (sharing multiple options),

They fear feedback from their manager even when they genuinely want to grow,

And how well they are liked within a group gives their voice weight in important decisions.

In other words, because human nature puts emotion before reason and logic, good logic and ideas can, paradoxically, only be built on a psychologically safe environment.

## Endless metacognition

Apparently, the correlation between doing assigned work well and years of experience is not particularly strong.

Just as people do not become master toothbrushers despite brushing several times a day, expecting skill to improve through time alone makes no sense.

The book divides self-improvement with compounding returns into three levels.

A work: simply doing the assigned task,

B work: improving the process of A work,

C work: improving the thinking and interactions involved in B work

The idea is to repeat these three routines continually, increasing the knowledge and abilities you acquire exponentially (through compounding).

Thinking beyond simply 'doing' to ask 'how' and 'what' is metacognition, and we must keep consciously practicing and repeating it.

I thought this was very important because, both in development with its vast sea of knowledge and in daily life, modern people (myself included) seem caught in Knowledge Obesity: taking in excessive information and knowledge without putting it into practice, and mistakenly believing we have learned something.

To overcome this, the book introduces 'active reading.'

Reading without specific questions or a purpose traps us in the goal of simply 'acquiring' information, leaving us unable to judge what matters and repeating the same mistake. Instead, we should clearly define the knowledge we want or the goal we aim to achieve, and approach reading as a tool for achieving it.

Then, once we judge during learning that we can achieve our purpose, we immediately stop reading and act toward the goal.

In fact, I was reading this book looking for counterarguments to the mentor who recommended it, and I think that was itself active reading.

I do think I was a little more absorbed, too...

## When asking an expert for advice

The book 'Peak' says that improvement always requires conscious effort, that repeated conscious effort becomes unconscious, and that we must then build new conscious effort on top of it.

In that sense, as experts repeat tasks and automate their processes, their learning process enters the unconscious realm, and they often cannot fully explain how they solve problems.

We ask them 'How do you do it?' and they inevitably respond with things like 'Work hard.'

Then how can I draw out the information I want from an expert—their way of learning?

I have to extract the thinking processes happening in the expert's unconscious.

Rather than simply asking 'What is your secret to learning programming languages quickly?', ask 'When you learned your most recent language, what did you do at each point in time?' and draw out the unconscious decisions and judgments hidden in the learning process, step by step through time.

## Anxiety is encouragement

Apparently, the gap between our level and what we are learning determines how anxious or bored we feel.

Ultimately, anxiety comes when we take on something harder than what we currently do, and can be called an inevitable emotion for broadening our perspective and making major progress.

We should acknowledge the anxiety itself without fearing it.
