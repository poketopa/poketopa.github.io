I thought that if there was a lot I did not know, I just needed to study.

But what made Woowa Tech Course difficult was not a shortage of things to study.

There were too many.

Trying to understand one thing uncovered something else I did not know, and looking that up required yet another concept. It was hard to distinguish whether figuring this out now mattered to the mission or merely satisfied my curiosity.

My slow pace could not be explained simply by a lack of knowledge.

Not knowing where to stop became a significant bottleneck.

## How much do I need to understand?

I used JDBC for the first time while connecting a DB to Janggi.

Previously, I had thought about how to translate behavior I understood into code. Now I first needed to understand why the objects appearing in the code existed at all.

Choosing a DBMS, connecting to it from Java, and storing data were all unfamiliar.

The `ConnectionFactory` interface in the code at the time was only a few lines long.

```java
@FunctionalInterface
public interface ConnectionFactory {
    Connection getConnection() throws SQLException;
}
```

But for me, the questions started with why this interface was necessary.

When I encountered a way of accepting the operation to execute in a transaction as a callback, that led me into functional interfaces and lambdas. I wrote this in [a reply on the Janggi PR](https://github.com/woowacourse/java-janggi/pull/358#discussion_r3081442522).

> Being able to accept and process the behavior itself greatly improved the code's readability, but I spent a lot of time understanding the lambda needed to call it.

I wanted to understand at least the code I wrote myself.

But there was a submission deadline, and the mission ran late. I was also told that I might not receive enough review.

Should I have requested a review once the code worked? Or was it right to spend time understanding my code as fully as possible?

I could not give a clear answer to that question in the Level 1 interview.

But I certainly feel that I need the ability to judge which information matters right now.

## Banning curiosity?

I had always tended to implement first and improve later.

I believed firsthand experience made knowledge my own, and when I did not understand something, I kept asking AI why.

This approach helped answer my questions.

But I would often dive deeply into one thing even when I needed a broad foundation. I spent a long time looking at small details before seeing the big picture I needed at that moment.

So I tried to restrain questions asked merely to satisfy curiosity, and set a fairly strict rule in my April personal learning assignment.

> Judge what matters and invest time only through AI and feedback

Looking at it now, that sentence pushes my own judgment too far aside.

But at the time, I was deeply anxious that I could not choose what mattered. I thought it was safer to spend time on what someone else told me was important than to keep digging endlessly on my own.

I also planned to organize what I learned as questions and try answering them the next day. I tried comparing new technologies with previous approaches, then added a reminder not to go too deep with that either.

## Stopping is a judgment too

Looking back, I think I expected that studying a lot would make this anxiety disappear.

But the more I knew, the more previously unseen unknowns came into view.

The question of how much I needed to know arose partly from insufficient study, but it was also a question I would have to carry with me as long as learning continued.

Even now, it is difficult to draw a line that applies to everyone.

But whatever choice I make, I want to leave a record of the reason.

Am I looking into this further now to actually change my judgment? Am I stopping because it is unimportant, or because it is difficult and I want to avoid it?
