## Introduction

As AI advances, we can obtain the knowledge we want, processed in the way we want, much faster than before.

If I want to, I can obtain most existing knowledge in an easy-to-understand form through search and AI.

In terms of tools, everyone now has an environment in which they could become an intellectual or scholar.

But as AI has advanced, have people actually become smarter?

Absolutely not.

Personally, two reasons come to mind.

## Knowledge like fast food

Humans instinctively like high-calorie foods, which once helped us survive.

But in the modern world, where food is not scarce, fast food can easily become surplus calories that satisfy our appetite and harm our health, rather than energy necessary for survival.

Knowledge is similar.

Humans instinctively enjoy acquiring new information, but exposure to enormous amounts of information through the internet and AI has not made us smarter.

Because what we enjoy is the moment our curiosity is satisfied and the feeling of understanding, rather than the information itself.

Whenever we discover a new fact through Shorts or social media, we feel the pleasure of having learned something, but we do not try to remember it, connect it to existing knowledge, or challenge it.

We simply look at information and consume it.

Studying must be distinguished from simply consuming information.

Studying does not end with encountering information.

It includes remembering it, connecting it to what we already know, questioning it, and trying to use it ourselves.

Simply consuming information quickly satisfies curiosity, but studying is an activity that requires paying a cognitive cost over a long period to gain understanding.

## Ignorance of our own ignorance

The second reason is that we do not know our own ignorance.

If I know exactly what I do not know, I can obtain most of the knowledge I need through the internet and AI.

But if I do not even know what I do not know, I cannot search for it or ask AI about it.

AI can answer questions, but it does not always tell me which questions I should ask.

Knowing what you do not know is itself an ability, and it varies according to upbringing, education, experience, and opportunity.

Even so, by questioning what we think we already know and continually identifying what we do not know, anyone can gain far broader knowledge than they have now.

This is the metacognition the book discusses.

But admitting our own ignorance is harder than it sounds.

The moment we admit that we do not know something, we face the burden of learning it anew and confronting our own shortcomings.

So rather than enduring the anxiety of not knowing, people try to believe they already know or cling to explanations they have believed in.

The Dunning–Kruger effect shows that people with less knowledge may rate their own abilities more highly.

People who do not know lack even the knowledge needed to judge what they do not know, making it difficult to discover their own ignorance.

Paradoxically, those who need learning the most may resist it most strongly.

If not knowing is taken to mean that we have less worth, admitting ignorance becomes even harder.

But the fact that I do not know something has nothing to do with a judgment that I am a worthless person.

Only by honestly admitting what we do not know can we approach learning with curiosity.

> The starting point is realizing and accepting that we are like fish that live in water yet do not know water
