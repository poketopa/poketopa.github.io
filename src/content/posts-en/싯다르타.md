Humans try to surpass others and become superior. Perhaps not everyone does, but I think it is human instinct, and I, too, have a strong desire for superiority.

I have countless standards for superiority, but three immediately come to mind: knowledge, appearance, and money.

I pursue intellectual vanity to know more than others, want to earn a lot of money to enjoy more than others, and work to look more attractive than others.

Money and appearance seem like simple things proportional to instinctive power, so I feel I simply need to obtain them.

But the nature of knowledge is fascinating.

The more deeply I consider philosophy, the stronger my belief that I should escape desire and attachment and find happiness rooted in myself.

The desire for knowledge comes to negate the other two.

From one perspective, perhaps that is because knowledge is the highest value among human desires.

But Siddhartha sees even the goal of pursuing knowledge and overcoming instincts and desires as a desire created by the human ego.

Pursuing money and fame may look different from pursuing enlightenment, but they resemble each other in trying to gain something and become someone different from who we are now.

Trying to surpass others and become superior eventually runs into a limit and ends in despair.

Then I have no idea what kind of life and attitude I should seek.

Siddhartha says love and connection matter most, but aren't those ultimately chemical reactions for passing on our genes?

This book leaves me with even more questions.

At least I found comfort in the idea that even suffering and sin are inseparable parts of a person's life, and that we should let them pass without allowing them to consume us.

Honestly, this kind of philosophy is gradually becoming a little overwhelming for me.

Humans seem to be insignificant creatures in the end, suffering in body and mind because of an awkward in-between state of evolution.

And in that process, I feel myself, once my whole world, becoming smaller and smaller.

We could call that growth, but according to Siddhartha, even growth might be a desire.

It is so difficult.

> We can find wisdom, experience wisdom, carry wisdom with us, and perform miracles through wisdom, but we cannot speak or teach wisdom.
