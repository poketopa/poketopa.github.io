## Precourse Week 1 retrospective

Last week, the Woowa Tech Course precourse began.

Trembling with anticipation and overwhelmed by the prospect of learning so much, I participated in community activities in various places.

The published Week 1 assignment was a simple number calculator. My honest first reaction was, "**This is too easy**..."

There were only a few lines of requirements and simple input/output examples. Compared with the algorithm problems I solved every day, it seemed far too easy.

But while writing the feature list in README.md, I realized that the quantity of requirements is by no means proportional to difficulty.

## Not 'solving a problem,' but 'creating a problem'

Coding test problems are kind.

They clearly provide every condition and constraint, and the solver simply follows that path.

But in this assignment, there were so many unclear considerations I had to decide for myself.

> What should happen if the user enters a non-numeric character?
 How far should custom delimiters be allowed?
What data type should I use for input?

There were no answers to countless questions. Those answers were entirely my responsibility.

I felt this was an assignment to '**complete**' a problem with my own logic and criteria, rather than '**solve**' one with a predetermined answer.

For me, the lack of a correct answer brought anxiety rather than freedom.

Even a small data type choice, like whether to use int or BigInteger,

> **What if my choice differs from the problem's intent? **

Made me worry so much that I spent days thinking without writing a single line of code.

Amid that uncertainty, a thought occurred to me.

> **What am I really supposed to learn in the precourse?**

I thought **communication** was what I could learn from this rare opportunity to work alongside so many people.

People say communication skills are vital for developers. Of course, lacking experience, I had not felt that firsthand.

Asking myself how I could communicate during the precourse led me to conclude that discussion is only possible if I can clearly articulate what I think.

To explain my code to others and convince them of my choices, above all I needed to answer "why?" clearly.

With that realization, I began focusing on developing sound reasons for my choices rather than finding a correct answer.

## Reducing ambiguity

First, I tried to treat README.md as a specification containing my design intent, rather than a simple feature list.

I wanted to design custom delimiters, input data types, and so on with my own intentions in mind.

I thought my priority now was to write code whose "why" I could explain, rather than high-quality code.

## Not getting discouraged

Just as I settled on a design direction, I hit another wall.

Unfamiliar concepts like MVC and TDD were being actively discussed in the community.

Others talked as though they were already familiar with them, while I did not even know exactly what the terms meant.

**Once again, impatience swept over me.**

But I did not want to follow others in applying those techniques without a criterion of "why?"

Instead, I thought the precourse was an opportunity to learn enough to answer those questions.

I gradually studied those development methodologies and conventions people mentioned, trying to understand and apply them in my code.

It was certainly not perfect or excellent code, but I created and submitted **code I could explain**.

## Finishing Week 1

The Week 1 mission, which I thought would be the simplest, posed the most fundamental questions about development.

Amid uncertainty, I established my own criteria, expressed them in code, and used tests to check what was wrong.

It was only a short week, but I learned an attitude toward code and a way of approaching problems.

This probably applies to every field, but I find it interesting that as my experience and skills in development grow, I instead start to see more shortcomings.

I am also incredibly excited for the rest of the precourse, where I can discuss and learn with others who are considering these same questions.

May my growth be boundless.
