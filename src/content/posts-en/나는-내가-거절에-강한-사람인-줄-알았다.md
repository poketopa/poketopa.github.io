I thought I was the kind of person who got over rejection quickly.

If someone turns down a request, can't I just accept that it cannot be helped? It is my request being rejected, not me as a person.

That was how I answered the questionnaire before the rejection workshop, too.

I gave myself a 5 on getting over rejection quickly, and a 1 on feeling inadequate when rejected.

Then I faced a lot of rejection while forming project teams. It was harder than I expected. I realized I did not know myself very well.

## When I was the one saying no

A fellow participant I was close to once suggested that we form a team.

I wanted to work with that person.

I just did not want to work on that project topic. My feelings toward the person had nothing to do with my judgment of the proposal.

This is what I actually said at the time.

> I'd like to work with you, but my team has just been finalized. I was going to tell you right away. I'm sorry.

I felt a little bad, but assumed they would understand.

From the perspective of the person saying no, there can be any number of specific reasons.

The topic may not be a fit, the timing may be wrong, or they may already have made another choice.

I thought both of us understood that distinction well.

## When I was the one being rejected

But when a team I applied to turned me down, I struggled to make that distinction.

I felt ashamed, inferior, disgusted with myself, and as though my abilities were lacking.

Before I could calmly analyze the reason, feelings about who I was came first.

The reasons for rejection I understood intellectually were quite different from how I actually experienced being rejected.

I learned that the size of a rejection is not determined solely by the length of the sentence or the other person's attitude.

What mattered was how much of my heart I had invested in that request.
