Recently, I recorded a book-discussion podcast with Woowacourse alumni.

The selected book was The Singularity Is Nearer, which argues that the singularity, when AI completely transforms our lives, has already begun.

The author speaks very positively about deeply integrating AI and technology into life, yet his ideas make me strangely uncomfortable.

It is a book worth considering when asking whether transforming human life through technology is right.

<p class="rilog-empty-block" aria-hidden="true"><br /></p>

Below are the discussion questions and my thoughts.

They are familiar philosophical questions, so hearing different opinions during the discussion was enjoyable.

<p class="rilog-empty-block" aria-hidden="true"><br /></p>

### Would you want to leave yourself behind after death as a replicant or a mind upload?<br />(If you could keep your memories after death by changing bodies, would you want to keep living?)

![Crayon Shin-chan Movie 22: Intense Battle! Robo Dad Strikes Back — Laftel, anime recommendations and streaming](/images/rilog/222/01.jpg)

<strong>I am against it.</strong>

Does the preciousness of life come from life itself?

Does life's meaning lie in the people, emotions, and memories we encounter as we live, in and of themselves?

<p class="rilog-empty-block" aria-hidden="true"><br /></p>

I do not think so.

Of course, that absolutely does not mean those things are meaningless.

But I think most of life's meaning is created by death.

Death makes life finite, and all value comes from that finiteness.

<p class="rilog-empty-block" aria-hidden="true"><br /></p>

<strong>Priorities only become visible when we feel that finiteness.</strong>

There was an experiment involving American university students.

<br />Asked what they wanted to do right now, they gave various answers.

Some wanted to rest at home alone; others wanted to spend quiet time with friends.

But when the question became what they wanted to do right now if they were going to die tomorrow, most answered that they would first go see their family.

In other words, remembering that there is an end, and getting closer to it, makes life's previously vague values and priorities clear.

<p class="rilog-empty-block" aria-hidden="true"><br /></p>

<strong>Then, conversely, what happens if the end becomes hazy and life approaches eternity?</strong>

Everything in our lives will lose its priority.

Everything we encounter, learn, and feel will lose its value.

Since nothing is bound to the one-time nature of “now,” there is no need to strive or rejoice.

Within eternal life, humans will obtain all knowledge and truth, all experiences and relationships, and forget the fear of loss.

A near-perfect state requiring no further change. A life in which that state is guaranteed.

<strong>How is that different from death?</strong>

Death, where the precious things we tried so hard to embrace crumble away, and eternal life, where the reason to embrace those things itself fades, seem to me not like opposites, but merely two different roads to death.

<p class="rilog-empty-block" aria-hidden="true"><br /></p>

#### Humanity is inevitably bound by instinct.<br />If brain programming lets us escape instinct and gain ultimate intelligence, does that make me a better version of myself?

![Arcane Savior Viktor | OP.GG](/images/rilog/222/02.webp)

<strong>I think it is meaningless.</strong>

<p class="rilog-empty-block" aria-hidden="true"><br /></p>

The book says brain programming can let us abandon unavoidable habits formed for survival.<br />Examples might include:

- Being drawn to sweets that damage our health (sugar was once hard to find in nature, but today it causes countless illnesses).
- Choosing small immediate pleasures over long-term goals (addiction to Shorts and Reels stems from this, too).
- Enjoying gossip for a sense of belonging (in the past, belonging itself was closely tied to survival).

<p class="rilog-empty-block" aria-hidden="true"><br /></p>

Anyone would want to abandon such habits.

They are instincts that once helped us survive, but have become obstacles to pursuing higher human values in the modern world.

<p class="rilog-empty-block" aria-hidden="true"><br /></p>

Yet the values we consider noble are deeply entangled with those same instincts.

Love, devotion, friendship, responsibility, filial affection, and belonging.

Arguing that we should abandon unavoidable habits created by instinct amounts to saying that even family relationships, bonds with others, and romantic love are byproducts of the instinct to pass on our genes—hormonal processes to be discarded for self-realization.

<p class="rilog-empty-block" aria-hidden="true"><br /></p>

<strong>Is that pleasant?</strong>

If you found it unpleasant, have you questioned whether that feeling, too, is merely a vague guilt engraved by instinct and education?

If love is nevertheless noble in a way those other things are not, what criterion distinguishes it from the examples above?

Is what we want to abandon instinct itself, or particular ways that instinct manifests?

<p class="rilog-empty-block" aria-hidden="true"><br /></p>

Individuals can have opinions on this. But there can be no answer.

In that sense, even the <strong>transcendence</strong> beyond instinct that the book pursues is only a desire built upon the human instinct to seek knowledge for survival. Even if we reach transcendence, these questions will arise again.
